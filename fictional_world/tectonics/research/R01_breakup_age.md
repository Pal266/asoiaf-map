# R01 — How long ago did the supercontinent start to break up?

**Question.** Decision 3 asks for a world "some tens of millions of years after a supercontinent began to break up:
many continents, long coasts, inland seas and rifts". Which age of breakup gives that picture? The Earth analogue is
Pangaea.

## Pangaea's breakup, stage by stage

- Pangaea was assembled by ~320 Ma (Leleu et al. 2015, Tectonics 34, 1009).
- Rifting in the Triassic; the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province at ~200 Ma; ocean floor in the central Atlantic from
  ~185–175 Ma. The first oceans, ~180 Ma: the central Atlantic and the south-western Indian Ocean (Britannica,
  Pangea; Schettino & Turco 2009, GJI 178, 1078).
- Karoo-Ferrar rifting (183 Ma) separates East and West Gondwana (Leleu et al. 2015).
- ~140–130 Ma: the South Atlantic opens; India leaves Antarctica and Australia (Britannica).
- ~95–80 Ma: the North Atlantic opens; Australia starts to rift from Antarctica (Britannica).
- By the end of the Cretaceous, most of today's continents have their modern shapes (HKU Stephen Hui Geological
  Museum).

| Time after the start of breakup | Pangaea's age then | What the world looks like |
|---|---|---|
| ~20–30 Myr | ~180–170 Ma (Early Jurassic) | one supercontinent with rifts and one or two narrow new seas |
| ~50 Myr | ~150 Ma (Late Jurassic) | two great continents (Laurasia, Gondwana) still largely whole; narrow oceans |
| **~80–100 Myr** | **~120–100 Ma (mid-Cretaceous)** | **many continents, young narrow oceans and fresh rifts, widest flooding of the continents (inland seas)** |
| ~150 Myr | ~50 Ma (Eocene) | continents dispersed, first collisions (India–Asia), sea level falling |
| ~200 Myr | today | wide oceans, new collision belts: the start of the next assembly |

## Why inland seas come with dispersal

- Young ocean floor is shallow, old floor deep (depth grows with the square root of age). After a breakup, a large
  share of the ocean floor is young, the ocean basins hold less water, and the sea floods the continents. Sea level is
  low when the continents are together and high when they are apart (Supercontinent cycle, Wikipedia; Worsley,
  Nance & Moody 1984; Nance 2022, Ann. N. Y. Acad. Sci. 1515, 33).
- In the Cretaceous the continents were widely flooded, followed by gradual regression of the inland seas. Müller et
  al. (2008, Science 319, 1357) model Late Cretaceous sea level 170 (85–270) m above today from ocean basin volume
  (that figure includes the melting of today's ice sheets). Miller et al. (2005, Science 310, 1293) give a long-term
  Cretaceous peak of 100 ± 50 m. Using today's hypsometry, 170 m is ~24 million km² of extra flooded land
  (Harrison et al. 1981, EPSL 54).

## Conclusion

The picture of decision 3 matches Pangaea ~80–100 Myr after its breakup began, in the mid-Cretaceous. **Decided (decision 6): the
breakup began 100 Myr before the present of the world.** The tectonic simulation then runs from an assembled
supercontinent through 100 Myr of rifting and drift. The sea level follows from the ocean floor ages it produces.

Note: the world has today's Earth atmosphere (decision 2). Part of the Cretaceous highstand came from the absence of
ice sheets in a warmer climate. In this world that part depends on the ice layer, while the young-ocean part comes
from the tectonics.
